\section{Problem 1: Learning Word Embeddings from IIT Jodhpur Data}
%% ============================================================

\subsection{Task 1: Dataset Preparation}

\subsubsection{Data Sources}
Textual data was collected from the IIT Jodhpur official website (\texttt{iitj.ac.in}) using Python's \texttt{requests} and \texttt{BeautifulSoup}. Data was gathered from:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Department \& Academic Program Pages:} All departments (CSE, EE, ME, Mathematics, Physics, Chemistry, HSS, etc.), academic programs (B.Tech, M.Tech, M.Sc, Ph.D), and research pages.
    \item \textbf{Academic Regulation Documents (Mandatory):} Academic regulations, examination guidelines, grading system, and academic calendar pages.
    \item \textbf{Faculty Profile Pages:} Faculty listings and individual profile pages across departments.
    \item \textbf{Course Syllabi \& Miscellaneous:} Course catalogue, placement statistics, student activities, alumni, campus infrastructure, library, and about pages.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Preprocessing Pipeline}
The raw text was processed through:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Boilerplate Removal:} Script, style, navigation, footer tags, URLs, emails, and HTML entities stripped.
    \item \textbf{Non-English Text Removal:} Non-ASCII characters (Hindi/other languages) removed via regex.
    \item \textbf{Tokenization:} NLTK's \texttt{word\_tokenize} for robust handling of contractions and abbreviations.
    \item \textbf{Lowercasing:} All tokens converted to lowercase.
    \item \textbf{Filtering:} Stopwords, web boilerplate words, tokens $<2$ characters, and purely numeric/punctuation tokens removed.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Task 2: Model Training}

\subsubsection{Implementation Details}
Both CBOW and Skip-gram with Negative Sampling \cite{mikolov2013w2v} were implemented \textbf{from scratch} using PyTorch \cite{paszke2019pytorch}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vocabulary Builder:} Word-to-index mappings; unigram distribution raised to $\frac{3}{4}$ power for negative sampling \cite{mikolov2013neg}.
    \item \textbf{Skip-gram:} Two embedding matrices (center and context). Loss: $\mathcal{L} = -\log\sigma(\mathbf{v}_c \cdot \mathbf{v}_w) - \sum_{k=1}^{K}\log\sigma(-\mathbf{v}_c \cdot \mathbf{v}_{n_k})$
    \item \textbf{CBOW:} Averages context embeddings to predict center word. Same negative sampling loss.
    \item \textbf{Optimizer:} Adam, lr = 0.001.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Task 1: Model Implementation}

All three models were implemented \textbf{from scratch} using PyTorch, sharing the same embedding and training procedure.

\begin{table}[H]
\centering
\caption{Shared Hyperparameters}
\vspace{-0.5em}
\begin{tabular}{lclc}
\toprule
\textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Value} & \textbf{Parameter} & \textbf{Value} \\
\midrule
Char Embedding Dim & 32 & Learning Rate (Adam) & 0.003 \\
Hidden Size & 128 & Epochs & 100 \\
Num Layers & 2 & Batch Size & 64 \\
Dropout & 0.2 & Gradient Clipping & 5.0 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsubsection{Model 1: Vanilla RNN}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Embedding ($51 \times 32$) $\rightarrow$ 2-layer Elman RNN (tanh, hidden=128) $\rightarrow$ FC ($128 \rightarrow 51$)
    \item Hidden state \cite{elman1990rnn}: $\mathbf{h}_t = \tanh(\mathbf{W}_{ih}\mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{W}_{hh}\mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{b})$
    \item Total Parameters: \textbf{61,971}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Model 2: Bidirectional LSTM (BLSTM)}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Embedding ($51 \times 32$) $\rightarrow$ 2-layer BiLSTM \cite{schuster1997bilstm} (hidden=128/dir) $\rightarrow$ Projection ($256 \rightarrow 128$) $\rightarrow$ FC ($128 \rightarrow 51$)
    \item Uses LSTM gates \cite{hochreiter1997lstm}; jointly trained unidirectional LSTM for autoregressive generation
    \item Total Parameters: \textbf{823,878}
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Model 3: RNN with Attention}
\begin{itemize}
    \item Embedding ($51 \times 32$) $\rightarrow$ 2-layer LSTM (hidden=128) $\rightarrow$ Bahdanau Attention \cite{bahdanau2015attention} $\rightarrow$ FC ($128 \rightarrow 51$)
    \item Attention: $\text{score}(\mathbf{h}_t, \mathbf{h}_s) = \mathbf{v}^\top \tanh(\mathbf{W}_1\mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{W}_2\mathbf{h}_s)$, \quad $\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \text{softmax}(\text{scores})$, \quad $\mathbf{c}_t = \sum_s \alpha_s \mathbf{h}_s$
    \item Combination: $[\mathbf{h}_t; \mathbf{c}_t] \rightarrow 128$ via linear + tanh
    \item Total Parameters: \textbf{289,043}
\end{itemize}

\centering
\caption{Model Parameter Comparison}
\vspace{-0.5em}
\begin{tabular}{lrr}

\textbf{Model} & \textbf{Parameters} & \textbf{Final Loss} \\
\midrule
Vanilla RNN & 61,971 & 1.4325 \\
BLSTM & 823,878 & 0.0008 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\midrule

\textbf{Analysis:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vanilla RNN:} Best balance --- 47.5\% novelty with 88\% diversity. Average length (6.0) closely matches training data (6.2).
    \item \textbf{BLSTM:} Very high novelty (99.5\%) but shorter, less realistic names. Lower diversity (75\%) indicates repetitive patterns. Extremely low training loss (0.0008) indicates overfitting of the bidirectional path.
    \item \textbf{RNN + Attention:} Lowest novelty (5\%) --- essentially memorized training data. However, generates the most realistic names with excellent length matching (6.1 vs 6.2).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Realism Assessment}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vanilla RNN:} Mix of realistic (Varsha, Narayan, Siddhi, Anika) and novel-but-plausible names (Jasneen, Mahadeep). Captures Indian phonotactics well.
    \item \textbf{BLSTM:} Shorter, experimental names. Some plausible (Kara, Zora, Pia) but many lack Indian characteristics (Poat, Noac, Doic). Bidirectional/unidirectional mismatch limits quality.
    \item \textbf{RNN + Attention:} Most realistic --- many are actual training names (Gajanan, Meera, Kavita). Attention provides strong character-level coherence.

\subsubsection{Discussion}

The three models represent a novelty--realism trade-off:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vanilla RNN} offers the best balance; its limited memory naturally introduces creative variation.
    \item \textbf{BLSTM} generates the most novel names but sacrifices realism due to the bidirectional training vs.\ unidirectional generation mismatch.
    \item \textbf{RNN + Attention} produces the most realistic names but largely memorizes the training set, leaving little room for creativity.
\end{itemize}
For practical use, the Vanilla RNN with temperature $\sim$0.7--0.9 provides the best novel yet realistic Indian names.

\section{Conclusion}
%% ============================================================

\textbf{Problem 1} demonstrated that meaningful word embeddings can be learned from a small, domain-specific corpus. Both CBOW and Skip-gram captured semantic relationships between IIT Jodhpur terms, and the from-scratch implementation matched Gensim's results.

\textbf{Problem 2} showed that character-level RNNs can learn Indian name phonology. The Vanilla RNN achieved the best novelty--realism balance, the attention model excelled at realism but memorized training data, and the BLSTM faced challenges due to the bidirectional--unidirectional generation mismatch.
